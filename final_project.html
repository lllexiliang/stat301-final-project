Introduction¶

Background & Literature Review

One of the most frequently debated topics of conversations nowadays, especially in North America, is tipping culture, where servers' income significantly relies on the tips amount. Questions such as How much is it appropriate to tip?, Who should tip more?, Should the value of your total bill amount affect how much you tip? are among the most commonly asked ones, both by customers, and by researchers, government, and servers.

Several studies have examined the patterns and trends of customers' tipping behavior, identifying a variety of factors that have proven to influence their tipping decisions. Lynn and McCall (2000) have discussed how service quality positively correlates with tipping amounts, alongside other factors, such as customer moods and social norms, and how societal expectations are mostly driving the tip amount rather than the service evaluation. On the other hand, Rogelberg et al. (1999) identified different types of factors that have been shown to influence the tipping decisions of customers, such as restaurant cleanliness, atmosphere, gender of the server, amount of alcohol consumed, total bill amount, as well as customer demographics. With all these studies, we can observe the multifaceted nature of tipping culture, alongside numerous social elements that influence tipping behavior, leading us to further analysis of real-world restaurant tipping dataset analysis.

Data Description

Our project aims to explore the tips dataset collected by a popular restaurant over a specific period of time (location and date were not explicitly stated), with the purpose of analyzing existing factors that influence tipping culture in this restaurant. It includes $244$ observations - unique transactions with $7$ different input variables: the total bill amount, tip given, the gender of the person paying, smoking preference, day of the week, time of day, and the size of the dining party.

Data Source: Badole, S. (2024, June 3). Restaurant tips dataset. Kaggle. https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/saurabhbadole/restaurant-tips-dataset/data

  1. total_bill: numerical variable representing the total amount of the bill in dollars ($)

  2. tip: numerical variable representing the total amount of the tip in dollars ($)

  3. sex: categorical variable representing the gender of the person paying (Male / Female)

  4. smoker: categorical variable representing the smoking preference (Yes / No)

  5. day: categorical variable representing the day of the week (Thursday / Friday / Saturday / Sunday)

  6. time : categorical variable representing the time of the day of the meal (Lunch / Dinner)

  7. size : numerical variable representing the size of the dining party

Research question: "How are the size of the dining party, the gender of the person paying, smoker status of customers, time and the day of the week associated with the tip percentage of the customer's bill in this popular restaurant?"

  • Response variable: tip percentage = tip / total_bill × 100 (%)
  • Explanatory variables: size, sex, smoker, time, day

Methods: Plan¶

We decided to use forward selection to build a linear regression model that infers tip percentage using a set of relevant explanatory variables. Forward selection method allows us to identify and select only the most relevant variables while maintaining model interpretability.

Method Reasoning:

  • Interpretability - unlike Lasso or Ridge, forward selection offers a clear, step-by-step view of variable inclusion, making it easier to observe and justify the significance of each explanatory variables included in the model.
  • Effective method for low-dimensional data - with only five candidate explanatory variables, forward selection method is both computationally efficient and avoids unnecessary model complexity.
  • Overfitting prevention - by stopping variable inclusion when additional variables do not significantly improve model performance, forward selection helps prevent overfitting the model, which is one of the main concerns for us in this setting, given our limited sample size and number of explanatory variables.

Method Assumptions:

  • Linearity: Method assumes a linear relationship between explanatory variables and response (tip percentage) variable.
  • Independence: Method assumes independent observations (e.g., tip percentage is not affected by other customers or previous tip percentages of same customer).
  • Normality of Residuals: Method assumes that the residuals are approximately normally distributed.
  • Homoscedasticity: Method assumes that variance of residuals is constant across all levels of the explanatory variables.
  • Multicollinearity: After applying the method, we will check VIFs to ensure no significant multicollinearity among explanatory variables.

Method Limitations & Weaknesses:

  • Non-linear Relationships: If the relationship between the explanatory variables and the response variable is in reality non-linear, the model will fail to capture important patterns, resulting in biased results.
  • Overfitting Risk: Despite forward selection's built-in stopping rule when adding new variables isn't improving model's performance, the small sample size still has overfitting risk, and it may retain variables that don't generalize well to new data.

Feature Selection

We decide to use Forward selection method with all explanatory variables in order to identify the most significant variables. As previously mentioned, this feature selection method adds one variable at each step, then evaluates the model's fit based on the change in adjusted $R^2$, $p$-value, or sometimes even change in $R^2$. This approach allows us to keep the most significant variables, whilst maintaining model simplicity and interpretability. After that evaluation, we selected that most improved the adjusted $R^2$.

Discussion¶

Interpretation of Results¶

Our final multiple linear regression (MLR) model, developed using forward selection, included sex and day as explanatory variables for predicting tip percentage. However, the model's performance was poor: it explained only 0.6% of the variation in tip percentage, and neither predictor was statistically significant. This suggests that the selected demographic and contextual variables have limited explanatory power for tipping behavior in this dataset.

Summary of Findings¶

The findings were somewhat unexpected. Based on prior literature and social intuition, we anticipated that factors such as gender of the payer or the day of the week might be associated with tipping behavior. However, our model showed that these variables do not meaningfully predict tip percentage. This result reflects the complex and likely subjective nature of tipping decisions, which may be influenced by psychological, situational, or cultural factors not captured in this dataset.

Model Improvement¶

To improve the model, future analyses could benefit from a richer dataset with more explanatory variables—such as service quality, customer mood, income level, or interaction terms (e.g., gender × time of day). Additionally, exploring nonlinear models or using regularization methods (like LASSO) could reveal subtle patterns that forward selection and linear regression may miss. Including interaction terms may also help uncover conditional relationships between variables.

Future Research Directions¶

This project highlights the limitations of inferring tipping behavior from a narrow set of contextual variables. Future research could incorporate survey-based or experimental designs to collect more granular and subjective data, such as customer attitudes or perceived service quality. Additionally, a cross-cultural comparison might provide insight into how societal norms shape tipping behavior across different regions.
